Choose a DEX for a Swap That Clears Safely and Cheaply

What to check before choosing a DEX for a swap: compare network and token support, total cost after fees, MEV protection and slippage for the trade you plan to make.

Crypto Bulletin Newsroom Nov 25, 2026 · 13:50 UTC 2 min read

Before choosing a DEX for a swap, check network and exact-token support, the amount you will receive after fees and network costs, and how the DEX handles MEV; quotes alone hide costs and execution risks that vary by venue.

cowswap is a decentralized exchange interface that uses signed trade intents and solver-run batch auctions. The linked idea of coincidence of wants adds context for how compatible trading intents can be matched.

What should you check before choosing a DEX?

Compare the same trade across DEXs, including Uniswap, 1inch and cowswap, using this checklist:

  • Network support. Confirm the DEX supports the network your wallet is using and where the tokens you plan to trade are held. A token with the same name on another network is a different asset for this swap; choosing the wrong network can leave you without the token you intended to trade.
  • Token contract address. Match the address displayed by the DEX against the issuer’s official address. Tokens with the same ticker can have different contract addresses, and copycat tokens can imitate a familiar name. The address is a more reliable identifier than the ticker alone.
  • Total cost after fees. Enter the same amount and compare the estimated amount received after the DEX’s fees and network cost. Network fees can change with congestion, while the exchange rate and fees can differ by venue. Compare the final amount for your wallet, not just the headline quote.
  • MEV protection. Consider how each DEX handles the risk of a swap being seen and acted on before it settles. A public-mempool swap with set slippage can be exposed to a sandwich attack. Intent-based DEXs such as cowswap settle through batch auctions, which limit sandwich attacks.
  • Slippage setting. Review the maximum price movement you allow before the swap fails. A tighter setting can prevent execution at a worse rate than expected, but a volatile market or thin liquidity may cause the trade to revert. A wider setting may let it execute, while allowing a less favorable price.
