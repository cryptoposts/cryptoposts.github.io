Intent-based swaps cut sandwich risk; approvals still matter

Is cowswap safer than Uniswap? For routine swaps, it usually lowers sandwich risk through batch auctions; approvals, phishing and fake tokens remain your responsibility.

Crypto Bulletin Newsroom Nov 27, 2026 · 03:17 UTC 3 min read

After a first swap, cowswap usually gives you less exposure to sandwich attacks than a standard Uniswap swap, but it does not remove approval, phishing or fake-token risks. That makes it a lower-risk choice for execution on Ethereum and L2s, not a guarantee that every trade or interface is safe.

How cowswap changes execution risk

In the protocol’s documented design, an order starts as an EIP-712 signed message, then competing solvers try to settle it in a batch auction. A standard Uniswap swap is a public transaction visible in the mempool before it is included, where searchers can spot a pending trade and try to sandwich it.

A sandwich attack needs that visible pending swap. These signed orders do not enter the mempool as swaps, so searchers cannot target them in the same way. To try this execution model, use cowswap; its batch auctions make it a concrete example of how orders can be grouped before settlement.

The protocol’s auction rules give trades of the same token pair in one batch a uniform clearing price. That matters because a solver cannot profit from reordering those traders against one another: they settle at the same price, rather than at a sequence of prices that a sandwich could exploit. Solver competition chooses a settlement, but the order’s signed conditions still constrain what can execute.

Approval and identity risks remain. A token approval gives a spender contract permission to move tokens up to the approved amount; on Ethereum, the relevant spender may be GPv2VaultRelayer. A fake front end can request a dangerous approval or signature, and a copycat token can use the same name and ticker as the real asset. Those risks apply whichever swap interface you use.

Reduce them by bookmarking the official domain, approving only the amount you intend to trade, and verifying the token’s contract address on the correct network. If you signed something unexpected, stop using that page, review the wallet’s recent signatures and transactions, and revoke any suspicious token allowance through a trusted allowance-revocation tool. A revocation is an on-chain transaction; it does not undo a transfer that has already happened.

What to know before signing or revoking

Does the interface hold my funds during a trade?

For a standard signed order, the order is an instruction to settle under its terms; it is not itself a transfer into a trading account. Token movement happens through the protocol’s settlement contracts when an order executes. Native-token flows can work differently, so review the transaction details before signing.

Can a solver steal my tokens?

A solver does not receive your token approval directly. The protocol’s documentation says the settlement contract checks your signed order and its limits, while the vault relayer handles approved token transfers. Still, only approve the intended spender and amount: a broad allowance creates more exposure if the spender or a connected interface is compromised.

Does Uniswap have MEV protection?

UniswapX uses signed orders and filler competition, so its execution path differs from a standard public Uniswap swap. Protection depends on the route and execution; do not assume every Uniswap swap avoids public-mempool exposure.

How do I revoke a token approval?

Use a trusted allowance tool or your wallet’s approval controls, select the correct network and token, then revoke the spender allowance you no longer want. Confirm the spender address before submitting the revocation transaction.
