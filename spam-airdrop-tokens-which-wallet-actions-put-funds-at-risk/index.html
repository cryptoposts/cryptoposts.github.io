Spam Airdrop Tokens: Which Wallet Actions Put Funds at Risk

What happens if you interact with a spam airdrop token? Receiving or viewing is usually harmless; signing, approving or trading can put assets at risk.

Crypto Bulletin Newsroom Nov 28, 2026 · 15:00 UTC 3 min read

A spam airdrop token is an unfamiliar token sent to your wallet; receiving or viewing it normally gives it no access to your funds, but links, signatures, approvals or trades can put assets at risk.

Say an unfamiliar token appears in MetaMask. Seeing its name or hiding it from your asset list does not approve spending or transfer other assets; hiding changes what the wallet displays, not what the address holds.

For the display-only step, see how to hide spam tokens before a Matcha swap. Hiding the token will not remove any existing allowance, so a Matcha swap or another site still needs to be assessed by the actions you took there.

Connecting shows your address; approvals grant spending access

Connecting MetaMask to a site can reveal your public address and the network you use. The site can then see public blockchain activity associated with that address, but the connection alone does not authorize it to move your tokens.

Signing is a separate action, and the request matters. A message signature may confirm an action or establish a permission; a transaction can directly change blockchain state. Read what the wallet says is being signed, and do not approve a request you cannot identify.

An ERC-20 approval gives a named spender permission to move that token up to the approved amount. The allowance may remain after you leave the site, so review the token, spender and spending limit before approving. A broad or unlimited allowance can put that token at risk if the spender is malicious or compromised.

Trading a spam token can trigger a transaction or approval

Attempting a Matcha swap may prompt a token approval before the swap transaction. A displayed price or swap quote is not itself a completed trade: inspect each wallet request, and reject it if the token, spender, amount or transaction details do not match what you intended.

If you only received, viewed or hid the token, you generally have not granted it spending access. If you connected to a site, review connected sites if you want to remove that connection; if you signed or approved something, check the wallet’s permissions or token allowances for the relevant network and revoke an unfamiliar allowance where available.

If you confirmed a transaction, review its details and the token allowance separately. The risk depends on the specific site, request, approval and token contract; a connection entry alone does not tell you whether a spender can move funds.
