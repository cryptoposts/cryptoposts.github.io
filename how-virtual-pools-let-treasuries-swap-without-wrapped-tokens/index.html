How Virtual Pools Let Treasuries Swap Without Wrapped Tokens

Virtual pools record cross-chain trades on a coordinating chain and settle from vaults holding native assets, so treasuries can swap without wrapped tokens.

Crypto Bulletin Newsroom Sep 29, 2026 2 min read

Virtual pools avoid wrapped tokens by recording trades on a coordinating chain and settling them from vaults that hold the original assets. A treasury can send one native asset into the system and receive another native asset on its destination chain, without minting an IOU token to bridge the two.

How does a virtual pool handle a treasury swap?

A virtual pool keeps the trade’s balances and pricing logic in protocol accounting rather than in a smart contract holding both sides of a token pair on one chain. Chainflip, for example, runs its Just-In-Time automated market maker on its State Chain, while its vaults hold assets on supported blockchains.

When a swap arrives, the protocol records the deposit, executes the trade against available liquidity, then arranges delivery of the destination asset from a vault. That lets the pool coordinate a trade between assets that do not share a blockchain. A breakdown of Chainflip’s pool and network swap fees covers the cost side that treasury teams should include when comparing routes.

Why does this avoid wrapped tokens?

A conventional cross-chain route may lock an asset on one chain and issue a representation on another. That representation depends on the bridge or custodian maintaining the link between the token and the locked asset. A virtual pool does not need to create that intermediate token: it tracks the trade internally and pays out an asset already held in a destination-chain vault.

The distinction is about where the swap is accounted for and what the user receives. The virtual pool still needs real liquidity on the relevant chains; “virtual” describes the trading layer, not the underlying assets. For a treasury, the operational result is that the destination wallet can receive the chain’s native asset rather than a wrapped version, if the protocol supports that asset and route.

What should a treasury check before using one?

A virtual pool can simplify the asset path, but it does not remove execution costs or reliance on the protocol’s settlement design. The treasury should confirm the destination asset, expected output, route liquidity and withdrawal costs before approving a swap.

Check these items in the quote and transaction details:

  • Asset identity: Confirm the output is the intended native asset on the destination chain, not a token with a similar ticker.
  • Liquidity and price impact: Larger trades can consume more pool liquidity and receive a worse price. Compare the quoted minimum output with the treasury’s tolerance.
  • Fees: Account for pool charges, protocol fees where applicable, and gas or broadcast costs on the source and destination chains.
  • Settlement controls: Check how the protocol controls its vaults, processes deposits and handles failed or delayed transfers.

The key trade-off is straightforward: virtual pools can route native assets without a wrapped-token hop, while shifting the accounting and settlement work to a coordinating protocol and its vault system. For treasury operations, the useful question is whether the native-asset route’s liquidity, fees and settlement model fit the mandate.
